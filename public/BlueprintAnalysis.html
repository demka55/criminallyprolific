The Getaway Car → Steal One Hour: Head-to-Head

Every meaningful element of the book, side by side. This is the full diff.

The Getaway Car (v1) Steal One Hour (v2)
Title The Getaway Car — evocative, cinematic, no unit of change baked in. Also: a 2014 Westlake book (Univ. of Chicago Press). Steal One Hour — verb + unit + micro-conflict. The verb "steal" implies taking from somewhere (the company). The unit (one hour) makes the promise concrete and testable.
Subtitle How a Soviet Immigrant Said No to Google, Sold Four Companies, and Reclaimed a 3-Hour Workday — author's story, not reader's promise. How founders who already won get their lives back without selling, quitting, or breaking the company — reader-first. "Already won" is the qualifier that selects the right person.
Category Business memoir. Crowded. No clear shelf position. Founder time-reclamation. Category-of-one: adjacent to productivity (but doesn't add time) and adjacent to exit planning (but doesn't require selling).
Big problem Working 8+ hours even though you've built companies. True but generic. No name. Founder creep — the founder identity expands every year until it fills every hour you have. Named, specific, sticky. Readers repeat it to friends.
Villain None named. "The grind"? Unclear what the book is against. Freedom later — the lie every founder tells themselves. "When we hit $X I'll have time." The villain is a phrase founders already use. Recognition is instant.
Hero (reader) Boris (primary) + Betsy (secondary). Two readers dilutes the positioning. Boris only. The whole book is written for and about Boris. Betsy appears once in a late chapter as a named example.
Boris profile Present but underused — described, not dramatized. No day-in-the-life hell scene. 72-hour weeks, loves Paris and piano and tango and bonsai. He works every minute. He calls it dedication. The book calls it founder creep. The heaven: Boris leaves at 4pm on a Tuesday. His phone doesn't ring.
Opening / First 15% Foreword: Clayton Christensen story, school pickup, "building prisons with great branding." Good writing, but The Dare and best story asset both buried past the 15% mark. Page Before Page One: The Dare is the first thing the reader does. Tuesday count. Leave one hour early. Write what broke. School pickup arrives in Ch. 5 as the narrative proof after the reader has already done the experiment.
The Dare Not present. School pickup story was the implied dare but never stated as an action the reader takes. "Leave one hour early tomorrow. Tell no one. Write down what broke." A concrete action on the first page. The book is built around proving the list will shrink.
Vocabulary (identity) None coined. No terms for the reader to repeat. Identity test: failed. founder creep · freedom later · your Tuesday · stolen hour · anxiety work · nothing broke · The Page · The Dare — 8 terms. Each one is repeatable at a dinner table.
Dinner-table test "What's the book about?" — "It's about how I built companies on 3 hours a day." Personal story. Hard to relay. "Founders who already won but can't stop working. You steal one hour. Nothing breaks. That's the whole book." Relayable in one breath.

Polarizing factor Too mild for 1-star reviews. No villain, no provocation, no claim that anything is wrong with how the reader currently lives. 3-star range. Heaven (reader's after-state) Vague: "reclaim time." Not visualized as a specific scene. Boris leaves at 4pm on a Tuesday. His phone doesn't ring. He goes to piano. The company is fine. He never knew it could be like this. Scene-specific, emotionally complete.

Three Tests Outside the Scorecard

Written for one real person

Hoehn's rule: narrow is wide. The book that tries to reach everyone reaches no one. v1 had two readers — Boris and Betsy — diluting the positioning. Fix: Boris only. Betsy appears in one late chapter as a named example. The whole book is written for and about Boris. His specific hobbies (Paris, piano, tango, bonsai) are named because they represent the life the company ate.

Boris — the bullseye reader

Dmitry's uncle. Software company co-founder, 14 years in, 72-hour weeks. Loves Paris, piano, tango, bonsai. Calls his schedule "dedication." The book calls it founder creep. When Boris finishes chapter one and does The Dare, and nothing breaks — that moment is the book working.

First 15% test

The average reader stops at 15% of a nonfiction book. v1 had the school pickup story — the best narrative asset — buried in Step 6. By the 15% mark, the reader was in framework-setup mode and hadn't seen the proof of concept yet. Fix: The Dare moves the proof to page one. School pickup is in Ch. 5. Reader sees the best story before they're 20% in.

The 15% line in the TOC

At ~300 pages, 15% = page 45. That's roughly through Ch. 2 "Freedom Later." The Dare happens before page one. The named villain (freedom later) arrives by page 45. The reader's own proof (school pickup story) arrives in Ch. 5 at ~25%. The first 15% now has: The Dare (action) → Ch 1 Founder Creep (naming) → Ch 2 Freedom Later (villain). That's the hook, the problem, and the enemy — all before page 45.

Polarizing

Great books get 5-star and 1-star reviews, never 3-star. v1 was too mild to generate either. No villain, no dare, no claim that anything is wrong with how the reader currently lives. Fix: "Founder creep" names a thing that was invisible. "Freedom later" calls out the lie founders tell themselves. The Dare is a provocation. Some founders will close the book. The right ones will finish it in one sitting.

Why "Steal One Hour" wins over the "one hour" shelf

Competitors: Power Hour, The One Hour Advantage, The Miracle Hour. All add an hour to the reader's day. This book takes one away from the company. The verb "steal" contains the entire differentiation. It also implies the company would object — which is the whole dramatic tension.
